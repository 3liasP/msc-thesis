\chapter{Research methodology}
\label{ch:research-methodology}

This chapter details the research methodology employed in this study. It focuses on the literature search strategy, the design science research (DSR) paradigm, and the single case study approach. The case context of Sovelia Core, an on-premise PLM system, is described, and the data collection and evaluation strategies used to assess the developed Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system are outlined.

\section{Literature search strategy}
\label{sec:literature-search-strategy}

The literature review for this thesis was conducted using a systematic search strategy to identify relevant academic and industry sources. The primary search platform was Andor \parencite{Andor}, Tampere University's library search service, which uses the Ex Libris Primo discovery system and the Central Discovery Index (CDI) in the background. The CDI has a relevance ranking algorithm that considers multiple factors, including term frequency, field weighting, citation counts, and publication recency \parencite{SearchRankingCDI2020}.

The search strategy combined domain-specific terms from PLM and enterprise software with emerging AI concepts, particularly RAG systems. Given the relative novelty of RAG technology in enterprise contexts, search terms needed to balance specificity (to find relevant work) with breadth (to capture the limited existing literature). Table~\ref{tab:search-terms} presents the search terms used and the number of results returned by each query.

The search strategy revealed a notable gap in the literature. While broad terms like ``AI'' AND ``assistant'' returned thousands of results, highly specific queries targeting RAG systems in PLM contexts (e.g., ``RAG'' AND ``PLM'') yielded only 13 results. This distribution reflects the state of RAG research in enterprise PLM environments and highlights the contribution of this thesis to an under-explored area.

For queries returning large result sets, sources were prioritized based on the Primo ranking algorithm \parencite{SearchRankingCDI2020}, which surfaces the most relevant and authoritative sources as the first results. This approach was particularly useful for broad searches like ``AI'' AND ``assistant'' (19 355 results) or ``artificial intelligence'' AND ``lifecycle management'' (1 300 results), where manual review of all results would be impractical. Conversely, for narrow searches yielding fewer than 100 results, a larger proportion of the returned sources was reviewed to ensure comprehensive coverage.

In addition to structured database searches, snowballing techniques were employed by following citations from key papers to identify seminal works in RAG architecture, vector databases, and enterprise AI deployment. Industry documentation, particularly from database vendors and LLM providers, supplemented academic sources to capture practitioner knowledge about deployment patterns and performance considerations. This industry literature is put to use in Chapter~\ref{ch:system-design-and-implementation}, where practical implementation insights are in the foreground.

The search strategy is best described as a structured and systematic literature search rather than a fully formal systematic literature review. This methodological choice was motivated by the novelty and fast evolution of RAG-related techniques in practice: relevant knowledge is currently distributed across peer-reviewed research, preprints, and vendor documentation. A pragmatic synthesis was therefore required to establish a credible conceptual foundation, and make sure that the selected design decisions remain feasible in an on-premise enterprise setting. Source selection emphasized relevance to domain specific deployment, data governance, and enterprise documentation retrieval, and it prioritized highly-cited and recent works in broad searches, while reviewing most results for narrow queries.

Peer-reviewed sources were preferred whenever available, and preprints were not treated as a default substitute for established literature. Where a peer-reviewed publication existed for a given preprint, the published version was selected. Two arXiv preprints were retained in the final reference list \parencite{gaoRetrievalAugmentedGenerationLarge2024, zhaoSurveyLargeLanguage2025} because they are comprehensive surveys that summarize rapidly evolving concepts and are frequently referenced as baseline overviews in recent work. Additionally, at the time of writing, these preprints are comprehensive, but still ongoing work of multiple iterations that continue to evolve. The limited number of directly relevant peer-reviewed sources, especially for RAG in enterprise PLM documentation contexts, also underscores the novelty of the topic and supports the significance of producing and evaluating a working artifact in an operational setting.

\section{Design science methodology}
\label{sec:design-science-methodology}

Design Science Research (DSR) is a problem-solving paradigm that seeks to create and evaluate artifacts intended to solve identified organizational problems \parencite{johannessonIntroductionDesignScience2021}. Unlike explanatory science, which aims to understand and explain phenomena, design science is prescriptive as it produces knowledge on how to design and build artifacts that serve human purposes. In the context of information systems research, these artifacts can take various forms, including constructs, models, methods, and instantiations.

According to \textcite{johannessonIntroductionDesignScience2021}, design science research follows a structured process consisting of five key activities: (1) \emph{explicate problem}, where the practical problem and its context are identified and defined; (2) \emph{define requirements}, which establishes the criteria that the artifact must satisfy; (3) \emph{design and develop artifact}, involving the actual construction of the solution; (4) \emph{demonstrate artifact}, where the artifact's utility is shown in solving the problem; and (5) \emph{evaluate artifact}, assessing how well it meets the requirements and solves the problem.

In this thesis, design science methodology is employed to develop an AI assistant integrated with Sovelia Core, an on-premise PLM system. The practical problem addressed is the knowledge accessibility challenge faced by users navigating fragmented documentation across vendor manuals, release notes, and customer-specific SOPs. The artifact in this case is the RAG system, which is designed, implemented, and evaluated through iterative cycles within both internal and external contexts. Internal environments, such as testing sandboxes, allow for rapid testing and refinement, while external contexts are mainly gathered through user feedback from demos and pilot implementations. The purpose of this approach is to contribute both a practical solution for Sovelia Core and generalizable knowledge about RAG system integration in on-premise enterprise PLM environments.

\subsection{DSR stages and connection to the thesis structure}
\label{subsec:dsr-stages-connection}

DSR was chosen because the research questions in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} are primarily design-oriented and prescriptive. RQ1 requires selecting and justifying an architecture under explicit constraints (on-premise deployment, PostgreSQL, governance). RQ2 focuses on engineering a maintainable process (documentation pipeline lifecycle). RQ3 concerns risk identification and mitigation in a real permission model. These aims align with DSR, where knowledge is produced through building and evaluating an artifact in context.

The \emph{explicate problem} stage is addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, where the documentation fragmentation problem and its motivation are defined, and in Section~\ref{subsec:case-description}, where the contextual constraints are described (on-premise deployment, sensitive engineering data, and heterogeneous documentation sources). These aspects define what is feasible and what constitutes success in this setting.

The \emph{define requirements} stage is accomplished by combining constraints from the case context with the literature in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature-review}. This results in concrete design goals for RQ1--RQ3, such as permission-aware retrieval, explicit source attribution, and version-aware handling of documentation so that the assistant can be governed similarly to the underlying enterprise system.

The \emph{design and develop artifact} stage is carried out in Chapter~\ref{ch:system-design-and-implementation}, which presents the architecture, the documentation pipeline, and the integration with Sovelia Core's permission model. This stage primarily contributes to RQ1 and RQ2, while implementing controls that mitigate the risks emphasized in RQ3.

The \emph{demonstrate artifact} stage is covered through end-to-end integration and representative query execution, showing that retrieval, access control enforcement, and response generation function as a coherent whole. This demonstration is reported across Chapters~\ref{ch:system-design-and-implementation} and \ref{ch:evaluation-and-results}.

The \emph{evaluate artifact} stage is addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation-and-results} through expert assessment of response quality and technical performance measurements. The evaluation is then interpreted against RQ1--RQ3 and synthesized into implications, limitations, and transferability in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.

The DSR process was iterative in the sense that implementation findings informed refinements to chunking, retrieval methodology, and risk controls. This was especially relevant for RQ2 and RQ3, where reliability and governance issues often emerge only when the pipeline is executed on realistic documentation and the assistant is exposed to diverse query types.

\section{Case study approach}
\label{sec:case-study-approach}

This research employs a single case study design \parencite{yinCaseStudyResearch2018} to investigate the integration of a RAG-based AI assistant within an on-premise PLM environment. The case study approach allows for in-depth exploration of the technical, organizational, and practical challenges inherent in deploying enterprise AI systems under strict data governance constraints.

The case study method was selected because the thesis is inherently commissioned and practice-oriented: the work was conducted in the R\&D context of Symetri and the Sovelia Core development team, where the primary outcome is a functioning artifact intended for real product development. In such a setting, the relevant design constraints are not abstract assumptions but operational realities, including on-premise deployment requirements, an existing permission model, heterogeneous documentation sources, and organizational risk tolerance. These contextual factors influence both what can be built (RQ1--RQ2) and what risks are critical in practice (RQ3), and they can only be assessed credibly by studying the artifact in its intended environment.

A single, information-rich case enables detailed examination of these constraints while supporting \emph{analytic generalization}: rather than claiming statistical representativeness, the case is used to derive transferable design principles for similar enterprise software development contexts, where documentation assistants must be integrated into existing systems under governance constraints.

\subsection{Case description: Sovelia Core PLM environment}
\label{subsec:case-description}

Sovelia Core is an on-premise PLM platform specifically beneficial for companies of varying sizes that are involved in product development and manufacturing. It is developed and maintained by Symetri, a provider of technology solutions catering to design, engineering, construction, and manufacturing businesses \parencite{Symetri2025Company}. The main purpose of the system is to serve as a centralized hub for managing product data, engineering change processes, bill-of-materials structures, and document management workflows throughout the product lifecycle. The system has a history of more than two decades and is widely adopted among various manufacturing enterprises in the Nordic region \parencite{DigitalSolutionsEngineering}. It is designed to be flexible, with implementations and features that can be adapted to the specific needs of both small and large organizations, from those needing basic document management to large enterprises with complex, global product lifecycles. Its scalability is demonstrated by its use in a wide range of industries and by companies with over 20,000 users worldwide. \parencite{DigitalSolutionsEngineering}

\subsubsection{Technical architecture and deployment model}

Sovelia Core follows a traditional client-server architecture \parencite{saternosClientServerWebApps2014} deployed entirely within customer premises. The system is built on a custom technology stack, with a PostgreSQL database serving as the primary data repository. Client applications connect to the server through proprietary APIs, and the system can be integrated with both Computer-Aided Design (CAD) systems (e.g. Autodesk Inventor \parencite{autodeskAutodeskInventorSoftware}) and various Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) platforms.

The on-premise deployment model is not just a technical preference but usually a fundamental requirement for Sovelia Core's customers. Manufacturing organizations in the mechanical engineering, industrial automation, and equipment manufacturing sectors typically handle sensitive intellectual property in the form of CAD models, proprietary designs, and confidential customer specifications. These organizations often operate under strict data sovereignty requirements, regulatory compliance mandates, or even contractual obligations that prohibit storing product data on external cloud infrastructure. Consequently, any AI-powered solution integrated with Sovelia Core must respect these constraints and operate entirely within the customer's controlled IT environment.

\subsubsection{User base and knowledge requirements}

The primary user base of Sovelia Core consists of mechanical engineers, product designers, production planners, and technical documentation specialists. These users interact with the system daily to access product information, manage engineering changes, release manufacturing documentation, and maintain synchronization between CAD designs and ERP data. User proficiency levels vary significantly, ranging from power users with deep system expertise to occasional users who require guidance for infrequent tasks.

A central challenge in this environment is the fragmentation of knowledge across multiple documentation sources. Users must navigate three distinct categories of documentation. First, \textit{vendor documentation} consists of official help files, user guides, and release notes provided by Sovelia, documenting system features, configuration options, and best practices. These include \textit{Sovelia Help} \parencite{SoveliaHelp}, an extensive online manual covering all aspects of the system, and periodic release notes in \textit{Sovelia.com} \parencite{DigitalSolutionsEngineering} that detail new features. Second, \textit{customer-specific documentation} consists of e.g., internal standard operating procedures (SOPs), workflow guidelines, and customization documentation created by each customer organization to reflect their specific business processes and system configurations. This knowledge fragmentation can create inefficiencies in user onboarding, increase support burden, and hinder user productivity. New users usually struggle to locate relevant information, while support staff face repetitive inquiries that could be answered through better knowledge accessibility.

\subsubsection{Data governance and security context}

Sovelia Core implements role-based access control (RBAC) \parencite{sandhuRolebasedAccessControl1996} to restrict data visibility based on user roles and organizational structure. Users should only access product data relevant to their functional responsibilities, and this principle must extend to any AI assistant that queries the system's knowledge base. Additionally, customer organizations usually have strict policies regarding data processing and AI usage. Many customers express concerns about data privacy, algorithmic transparency, and the risk of AI hallucinations leading to incorrect guidance in engineering workflows. These concerns shape the requirements for any AI integration, including source attribution, and mechanisms to mitigate the risk of fabricated information. The aim is to make the AI assistant cite sources clearly and provide verifiable references for all generated responses.

Another important topic is the cost-effectiveness of the solution. Many manufacturing organizations operate under tight budget constraints, making it essential that the RAG system delivers tangible productivity improvements without incurring prohibitive costs related to infrastructure, licensing, or maintenance. Based on historical customer feedback, solutions that are too costly or complex to adopt and maintain often face resistance during adoption even if they offer new advanced capabilities.

\subsection{Case study boundaries and unit of analysis}
\label{subsec:case-boundaries}

The unit of analysis in this case study is the \emph{integrated RAG system} as implemented within a Sovelia Core customer environment. This includes the technical components (embedding models, vector database, retrieval logic, LLM interface), the documentation pipeline (ingestion, chunking, embedding generation), and the user-facing interface integrated into the PLM platform. Sovelia Core itself is an excellent environment for studying RAG integration due to its roots being in the Document Management System (DMS) domain, where effective knowledge management has been one of its core features from the start.

The case study is bounded by three scope constraints. The temporal scope covers the design, implementation, and initial evaluation phases within a defined timeframe, while long-term adoption patterns and broader organizational change impacts are outside the scope. The technical scope focuses on RAG architecture, on-premise deployment constraints, and documentation retrieval, while general LLM capabilities, conversational AI design, and broader knowledge management systems are discussed only when directly related to the RAG implementation. The organizational scope acknowledges that organizational context shapes requirements and evaluation, but the study does not constitute a comprehensive organizational change management analysis.

\subsection{Data collection and evaluation approach}
\label{subsec:data-collection}

Data collection in this case study combines multiple sources to formulate the findings. The primary data consists of system implementation artifacts (such as architecture documentation and source code) that document design decisions and the implementation approach. Expert evaluation is used to assess responses generated by the RAG system during internal testing deployments, focusing on accuracy, relevance, and source attribution. In addition, technical performance metrics are collected, including token usage, response latency, and embedding generation time.

Expert evaluation is conducted by three Sovelia Core domain experts (a product manager, a senior application engineer, and a product expert responsible for Sovelia Help documentation). The evaluators and the assessment procedure are described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:evaluation-procedure}.

The source code itself will not be made publicly available due to intellectual property considerations. The evaluation strategy follows the design science framework's emphasis on \textit{technical performance} (whether the artifact functions as designed) and \textit{utility} (whether the artifact addresses the identified problem). Technical performance is assessed through qualitative RAG performance metrics, while utility is evaluated mainly through expert feedback.

\subsection{Case study and thesis structure}
\label{subsec:case-study-connection}

The case study also structures the thesis and reflects the commissioned development setting. Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} motivates the practical problem and defines the research questions from the perspective of an enterprise product development context. Chapter~\ref{ch:research-methodology} specifies the case boundaries, unit of analysis, and data sources, which include implementation artifacts produced during R\&D work (architecture documentation, pipeline configuration, and the resulting software) and evaluation data collected from internal testing and expert review.

Chapter~\ref{ch:system-design-and-implementation} describes the artifact's implementation in the Sovelia Core environment. It uses case constraints as design drivers. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation-and-results} presents empirical observations from the development setting. These include expert evaluations of response quality and performance metrics in realistic configurations.

The main outcome of this case study is a validated set of design choices that can be transferred to other document-heavy enterprise systems with similar constraints. These include using a relational database with vector extensions for governed retrieval, enforcing permissions at retrieval-time, treating the documentation pipeline as a versioned lifecycle process, and evaluating response quality with separable dimensions that distinguish retrieval failures from generation failures.
